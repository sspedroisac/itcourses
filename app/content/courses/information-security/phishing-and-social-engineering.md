# Phishing and Social Engineering

Many cyberattacks do not begin with sophisticated hacking techniques—they begin by **tricking people**.

**Social engineering** is the practice of manipulating individuals into revealing sensitive information or performing actions that compromise security. One of the most common forms of social engineering is **phishing**.

Because humans are often the weakest link in security, understanding these attacks is essential for everyone, not just IT professionals.

---

## What Is Social Engineering?

**Social engineering** is the psychological manipulation of people to gain unauthorized access to information, systems, or physical locations.

Instead of attacking technology directly, attackers exploit human emotions and behavior.

Common emotions exploited include:

- Trust
- Fear
- Curiosity
- Urgency
- Greed
- Sympathy

For example, an attacker may pretend to be a coworker, IT support, or a trusted company to convince someone to reveal confidential information.

---

## What Is Phishing?

**Phishing** is a type of social engineering attack where attackers impersonate a trusted source to trick victims into revealing sensitive information or installing malware.

Phishing attacks commonly aim to steal:

- Usernames and passwords
- Credit card numbers
- Banking information
- Personal data
- Multi-factor authentication (MFA) codes

Phishing is typically delivered through email but can also occur through text messages, phone calls, social media, or fake websites.

---

## How a Phishing Attack Works

A typical phishing attack follows these steps:

1. The attacker sends a convincing message.
2. The victim believes the message is legitimate.
3. The victim clicks a malicious link or opens an attachment.
4. The victim enters sensitive information or downloads malware.
5. The attacker gains unauthorized access.

Many successful cyberattacks begin with this simple process.

---

## Common Types of Phishing

### Email Phishing

The most common type of phishing.

Attackers send fraudulent emails pretending to be banks, online services, delivery companies, or coworkers.

**Example:**

> "Your account has been suspended. Click here to verify your password."

---

### Spear Phishing

**Spear phishing** targets a specific individual or organization.

Attackers often research their victims to create highly convincing messages.

**Example:**

An employee receives an email that appears to come from their manager requesting access to confidential documents.

---

### Whaling

**Whaling** targets high-profile individuals such as executives, directors, or business owners.

Because these individuals often have greater access to sensitive information, successful attacks can cause significant damage.

**Example:**

A fake legal notice sent directly to a company's CEO.

---

### Smishing

**Smishing** combines SMS (text messaging) with phishing.

Victims receive fraudulent text messages containing malicious links.

**Example:**

> "Your package couldn't be delivered. Track it here."

---

### Vishing

**Vishing** (voice phishing) uses phone calls instead of emails.

Attackers often impersonate:

- Bank employees
- Government agencies
- Technical support
- Company executives

The goal is to convince victims to reveal sensitive information or perform unauthorized actions.

---

## Other Social Engineering Techniques

### Pretexting

An attacker invents a believable story (a **pretext**) to obtain information.

**Example:**

Someone pretends to work in the HR department and asks an employee to verify personal information.

---

### Baiting

Attackers offer something attractive to lure victims into compromising security.

Examples include:

- Free software
- Free movies
- USB drives left in public places
- Fake giveaways

The "reward" often contains malware.

---

### Tailgating

Also called **piggybacking**, tailgating occurs when an unauthorized person follows an authorized employee into a restricted area.

**Example:**

An attacker carrying boxes asks an employee to hold the secure door open.

---

### Quid Pro Quo

An attacker offers a service or benefit in exchange for information.

**Example:**

Someone pretending to be IT support offers to fix a computer problem while asking for the user's password.

---

## Warning Signs of Phishing

Many phishing attempts share common characteristics.

Be cautious if a message:

- Creates a sense of urgency.
- Requests passwords or sensitive information.
- Contains unexpected attachments.
- Includes suspicious links.
- Has spelling or grammar mistakes.
- Comes from an unfamiliar sender.
- Uses generic greetings such as "Dear Customer."
- Asks you to bypass normal procedures.

Even professional-looking messages should be verified before taking action.

---

## How to Protect Yourself

Good security habits dramatically reduce the likelihood of becoming a victim.

Best practices include:

- Verify the sender before responding.
- Hover over links before clicking them.
- Never share passwords through email or text messages.
- Use Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA).
- Report suspicious messages to the IT or security team.
- Keep software updated.
- Be cautious with unexpected attachments.
- Confirm unusual requests using another communication channel.

When in doubt, verify first.

---

## Real-World Example

An employee receives an email that appears to come from Microsoft stating:

> "Your password expires today. Click here to keep your account active."

The employee clicks the link, enters their username and password on a fake login page, and unknowingly sends their credentials to an attacker.

The attacker then uses those credentials to access company systems.

This attack required **no technical vulnerability**—it relied entirely on human trust.

---

## Social Engineering vs. Technical Attacks

| Social Engineering                      | Technical Attack                               |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Targets people                          | Targets systems                                |
| Exploits trust and emotions             | Exploits software or hardware vulnerabilities  |
| Often requires user interaction         | May occur without user involvement             |
| Difficult to stop with technology alone | Can often be mitigated with technical controls |

Organizations need both technical defenses and security awareness training to defend against modern cyber threats.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Social engineering** manipulates people rather than technology.
- **Phishing** is one of the most common social engineering attacks.
- Attackers exploit emotions such as trust, fear, curiosity, and urgency.
- Common phishing types include email phishing, spear phishing, whaling, smishing, and vishing.
- Awareness, verification, and good security habits are among the strongest defenses against these attacks.
