# Functional and Non-Functional Requirements

Requirements describe what a system must do and what constraints it must satisfy.

They are often divided into **functional requirements** and **non-functional requirements**.

---

## Functional Requirements

Functional requirements describe specific behaviors or capabilities.

Examples include:

- Users can create an account.
- Customers can reset a password.
- Administrators can export a report.

These requirements define features and user actions.

---

## Non-Functional Requirements

Non-functional requirements describe qualities or constraints.

Examples include:

- The page should load in under two seconds.
- The service should be available 99.9% of the time.
- Data must be encrypted at rest.
- The interface should be accessible.

These requirements shape how the system behaves under real conditions.

---

## Why the Difference Matters

Two systems may have the same features but very different quality.

For example, two login pages may both allow sign-in, but one may be:

- Faster.
- More secure.
- Easier to use.
- More reliable under heavy traffic.

Non-functional requirements often determine whether software succeeds in practice.

---

## Common Mistake

Teams sometimes focus heavily on visible features and forget to define performance, security, reliability, or maintainability expectations.

This can lead to software that technically works but fails users in real-world situations.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Functional requirements define what the system does.
- Non-functional requirements define how well the system should do it.
- Both are necessary for successful software.
- Good engineering requires treating quality attributes as first-class requirements.
